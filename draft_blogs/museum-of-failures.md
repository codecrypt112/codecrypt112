# Museum of Failures — or, The Graveyard of Trying

*by Vikash V · July 2026*

---

Every developer has one.

Some call it `archive`.

Some call it `old_projects`.

Some don't bother naming it at all.

It's just a place where ideas go to die.

Mine isn't particularly organized.

It's full of projects that almost worked.

Projects that definitely didn't.

Ideas that made perfect sense at two in the morning and absolutely none the next afternoon.

Sometimes I open those folders.

Not because I want to revive them.

Just to remember who I was when I started.

---

There's a strange kind of optimism required to begin something new.

When you create a fresh repository, every outcome still exists.

Maybe this becomes a startup.

Maybe it becomes a research paper.

Maybe it becomes an open-source project people actually use.

Maybe it changes your life.

For a brief moment, every possibility is still alive.

Then reality shows up.

---

One project dies because someone else solved it better.

Another because you underestimated how difficult it would be.

One because life got busy.

One because your interests changed halfway through.

Another because, after two weeks of building it, you realized nobody actually needed it.

None of those reasons feel dramatic.

Most projects don't end with a catastrophic failure.

They simply... stop.

One commit becomes two weeks without opening the repository.

Two weeks quietly become a year.

---

For a long time, I hated looking at unfinished work.

It felt like evidence that I lacked discipline.

Now I see something else.

I see curiosity.

Every abandoned project is proof that I cared enough to begin.

Some ideas deserved to fail.

Some deserved a better version of me than the one who started them.

Some existed only to teach me one lesson before disappearing.

That's enough.

---

I remember trying to read papers I couldn't understand.

Building systems I had no business attempting.

Training models that produced nothing but errors.

Writing code that worked exactly once.

Back then, it felt embarrassing.

Now it's almost funny.

Because every project I consider "successful" today exists only because dozens of invisible ones failed first.

Nobody asks about those.

They ask about the final result.

---

Success has excellent public relations.

Failure doesn't.

People post screenshots when something launches.

Nobody posts the week they spent debugging a problem that turned out to be a typo.

Nobody announces they deleted five thousand lines of code because the entire design was wrong.

Nobody celebrates realizing they should start over.

Maybe we should.

Those moments are expensive.

They're also where most learning happens.

---

I don't think failure is the right word anymore.

Failure suggests something was supposed to succeed.

Trying doesn't make that promise.

Trying simply says,

*"This seemed worth exploring."*

Sometimes exploration leads somewhere.

Sometimes it leads to a dead end.

The dead ends matter too.

They teach you where not to build next.

---

Every now and then, I revisit an old project.

Not to finish it.

Just to read my own code.

It's like opening a time capsule.

You can almost hear the version of yourself who wrote it.

You notice the shortcuts.

The unnecessary complexity.

The comments that confidently explain something you now know is completely wrong.

It's oddly comforting.

Growth is easier to measure backwards.

---

Maybe that's why I don't delete those projects anymore.

They aren't clutter.

They're evidence.

Evidence that I kept showing up.

That I kept being curious.

That I was willing to look foolish in private so I could become a little better in public.

Not every repository deserved a release.

Not every experiment deserved a paper.

Not every idea deserved a second attempt.

But every one of them deserved to exist.

Because without them, I wouldn't have become the person capable of building the things that did survive.

---

I still start too many projects.

I still underestimate how hard they'll be.

I still convince myself,

*"This one will only take a weekend."*

It never does.

And honestly...

I hope I never stop.

Because one day, when I look back at my own little museum of failures, I don't want to remember everything I finished.

I want to remember that I never stopped trying.
